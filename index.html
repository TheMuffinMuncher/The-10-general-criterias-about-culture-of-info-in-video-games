Save Point Field Report Nº 07 — 2025 Edition

The information culture of videogames

The Informed Player

Ten criteria on how videogame knowledge is made, shared, guarded and lost — from the wiki to the datamine, the patch note to the dead server.

10 Criteria· — min read· Keys ← → navigate
Scroll
PreambleWhy this report

Before the first boss, there is the wiki.

For most of the medium’s short history, information about a videogame was scarce and official. It arrived in a printed manual, a magazine strategy page or a premium-rate hint line, and it ended there. Today the situation is inverted: there is more writing about any given game than there is game, and almost none of it is official. The knowledge that used to fit in a booklet now arrives as a living layer wrapped around the product — written by volunteers, contested in real time, and rewritten after every update.

Method Observation of public surfaces: wikis, patch feeds, datamine threads, chat servers, ladder statistics, fan archives.

This report collects ten working criteria — not laws, not predictions — for reading how videogame knowledge is produced, circulated, guarded, and occasionally lost. Read them as a field guide to the invisible game every player is already playing.

01 CriterionDocumentation
01

The wiki is the true manual

— A game is documented by the people who play it, or it is not documented at all.

Publishers stopped shipping manuals; players started shipping libraries. The authoritative reference for a modern game is a wiki — written, disputed, footnoted and re-verified by volunteers who care more about frame data than any documentation department ever could. Drop rates, dialogue trees, regional differences: the wiki knows what the manual never did.

Practices Fandom & wiki.gg farms · speedrun.com leaderboards · frame-data sheets · “citation needed” disputes.

This changes the status of the knowledge itself. Documentation is no longer a product that precedes play; it is a byproduct that trails it — provisional, versioned, and permanently under construction. The half-life of a wiki page is one patch.

02 CriterionCanon & versioning
02

Patch notes are living canon

— Every balance change is a published amendment to the rules of the world.

A videogame is no longer a finished object; it is a versioned one. Patch notes are consequently read the way legislation is: parsed line by line, debated, weaponised in arguments, and reverse-engineered for what they don’t say. The metagame moves on the day of the notes, before anyone has even played the build.

Specimen — patch-note style · illustrativeFig. 01
v7.3.1 — “Lantern” · Kestrel: sidestep invulnerability reduced by 4 frames · Fixed an issue where the Ashen Door could open twice · The Lantern’s debuff no longer applies through walls · (undocumented) the door now remembers being opened

Where the arcade tested changes silently and the disc shipped once and forever, the live game publishes its own history as it goes. To follow a game today is to follow a docket.

Practices Official forums · patch-note subreddits · datamine cross-checks.
03 CriterionSecrecy & leaks
03

The datamine ate the surprise

— Secrecy is now a question of encryption, not etiquette.

Every client shipped to a million machines is an archive waiting to be opened. Dataminers extract unreleased characters, events and maps weeks before any announcement, and the marketing calendar now races the file itself. Some studios obfuscate their assets; others have simply started announcing earlier. Either way, the trailer no longer reveals — it confirms.

Field note “The announcement is a formality; the files already told us.”

The result is a strange etiquette of spoiling: communities police what may be discussed in public threads, while the information itself sits unencrypted on every participant’s hard drive. Consent to know is negotiated daily.

04 CriterionThe press
04

The new press has a face and a chat

— Trust in coverage has become parasocial before it is editorial.

For a generation of players, reviews, investigations and commentary arrive as video from individuals, not institutions. A streamer playing a launch build is simultaneously review, advertisement and documentation — often all three within the same hour, with no disclosure borders between them.

Practices Day-one streams · video essays · creator early-access codes · drama reporting.

The old press hasn’t vanished, but the centre of gravity has moved to accounts with names instead of mastheads. Editorial standards vary accordingly: a video’s errata live in its comment section, sorted by engagement rather than accuracy.

05 CriterionOptimisation
05

Guides removed the friction — and some of the game

— Strategy is crowdsourced before the first hour is played.

Optimisation culture front-loads the ending into the beginning. Open a newly released game and the optimal build already exists — ranked, argued over, updated hourly. For many players, consulting it is not cheating but etiquette: the price of playing with others who did.

Field note “The difficulty toggle is now a browser tab.”

Designers have noticed. Some now build explicitly for the informed player, hiding depth for those who resist looking things up; others design as if the guide were part of the interface. Difficulty, meanwhile, has quietly become optional in a second way.

06 CriterionArchival dark matter
06

The deepest archive isn’t indexed

— The most current knowledge is the least preserved.

The liveliest game knowledge today lives in chat servers: a pinned message, a developer answering at 2 a.m. in a channel with forty thousand members, a frame-perfect discovery posted once and never again. None of it is reachable by a search engine. None of it will outlive the server it sits in.

Specimen — the exchange no search engine indexesFig. 02
[02:14] mantis_cult so the third hit of the chain IS cancellable, but only on whiff? [02:16] dev_kaz only on whiff, and only in the corner. it’s a bug. we’re keeping it. [02:17] mantis_cult incredible. documenting this now [02:17] mantis_cult pinned.

Future historians will find the wiki but not the conversation that corrected it. The dark matter of game knowledge is real-time, unindexed and — by the standards of every archive that preceded it — unsaved.

Practices Pinned messages · #patch-feedback channels · forgotten phpBB forums.
07 CriterionVerification
07

Rumour outruns verification

— A leak with a screenshot beats a fact with a source.

Leak culture runs on fragments: anonymous pastebins, blurry captures, an “uncle at the studio” economy that never fully dies. Communities respond with folk-verification — matching typefaces, checking file-naming conventions, weighing a leaker’s track record like a credit score.

Folk-checks Font forensics · filename conventions · leaker track records · archive.org snapshots.

The incentive structure, however, rewards being first rather than being right. A false leak costs its spreaders nothing; a missed one costs attention. Misinformation about games is low-stakes, which is precisely why it is high-volume.

08 CriterionPreservation
08

Preservation is fought, not granted

— A game that cannot be re-bought must be re-documented to survive.

Delisting, licence expiry, server shutdown: games now disappear while still installed on millions of machines. When a title vanishes from every storefront, its documentation effectively becomes the game — the only complete record of what it was.

Field note “Delisting deletes a storefront entry, not a game — unless nobody kept the files.”

So the community defends it: fan-made servers, archived clients, recorded network traffic, manuals scanned from rental copies. None of this is granted by the EULA. The right to remember a game is exercised, not given.

09 CriterionAnalytics
09

To spectate is to read a dashboard

— Watching a match is now half statistics, half story.

Esports arrives wrapped in live data: gold graphs, cooldown trackers, win probabilities recalculated every second. Outside the broadcast, ladder sites quantify the metagame hourly, and commentary quotes numbers the way sportscasters once quoted batting averages.

Specimen — the spectator’s second screenFig. 03
KESTREL — fighter · ladder week 34 pick rate ......... 18.2% ↑ win rate .......... 52.7% (elite 61.9%) ban trend ......... +9.4% since v7.3.1 worst matchup ..... 3–7 vs Ashen note .............. see criterion 02

The spectator is thereby also an analyst — fluent in pick rates and matchup spreadsheets, arguing not about beauty but about expected value. Watching has become a second, quieter game of reading.

10 CriterionHistoriography
10

Players have become historians

— The audience keeps the records the industry will not.

Version histories are reconstructed frame by frame from old captures. Trailers are archived before takedowns land. Lost builds are recovered from rental discs and developer hard drives, and fan museums curate what no institution has budget for. The medium’s memory is crowdsourced by default.

Practices Version archives · lost-media wikis · fan museums · personal ROM collections.

This is the quiet outcome of everything above: the wiki, the datamine, the indexless chat, the fan server. The most durable institution in game preservation is not a company — it is a player with a folder, a spreadsheet, and the stubborn belief that the save file should outlive the studio.

The booklet became a wiki. The rumour became a leak. The memory became an archive.

Ten criteria, one direction of travel: the culture of information around games has become the culture of the games themselves. Whatever this medium will keep of its first century, it will keep through its players — documented, disputed, and patched, like everything else we love.
